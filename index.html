Where this is going

the long-term goal is an agent that works like a mechanical engineer. you describe the part you need and the loads it has to carry. the agent designs it in cad, runs the simulations an engineer would run (stress, deflection, heat, fatigue), changes the design when it fails, and only hands it over once the numbers pass. what you get is a file you can manufacture, plus the test results that show it works.

robots are the first obvious user. a robot that needs a new bracket, gripper finger or mount could get one designed and checked without waiting for an engineer. that's a step towards robots designing and building parts for other robots.

the use case i think about most is space. on a space station, or later on the moon or mars, a crew can't wait months for a replacement part from earth. with a 3d printer or machine on board and an agent like this, they could describe the broken part, get a design already simulated against the loads it will face, and make it on the spot.

none of this exists yet. datak's data and the benchmark are the first two steps, because you can't train a model like this, or trust it, without good data and a fair way to test it.

What i've learned

  • plan distribution while you build the product. the blip features that sold were the ones i could explain in a one-line reddit post.
  • run a cheap version of the experiment yourself instead of reading about someone else's. a lot of growth advice is one founder's experience written up as a rule.
  • if you'll do something twice, automate it. a scheduled workflow is more reliable than relying on motivation.
  • the unglamorous parts matter most. retry logic, dedupe keys, rate limits and clear limits on what a system can do are what let you leave it running. i learned this building pipelines, and again when blip went down.
  • being the youngest in the room mattered less than i expected. people cared whether the numbers in the dashboard were right.
  • ask for help early and in public, though the answer usually comes from someone you already know. i waited a full day before posting about the blip outage, and that delay cost more than the embarrassment would have. the fix came from a colleague who lent me his api account, not from the 3,372 people who saw the post.
